Refugee Empowerment Network, a registered NPO in Tokyo

Each person has an individuality, a charm, and a life that the word refugee alone cannot show.

Aiming for a society where everyone is respected as a person, lives in security, and can make use of their own strengths. Such a society would surely be far more wonderful than the one we have now.

Cultural exchange

Centered on learning Japanese, REN continues efforts to support each person's daily life through various activities and partnerships.

Bead accessories and craft projects

Each accessory, made with care, has reached many people through bazaars, events, and more.

Scholarship support

Each year, REN gives a small scholarship for higher education to about two refugee students, so that one day they can build a career in their home countries.

What REN is doing now

Japanese lessons, and time to share cultures

REN is run entirely by volunteers. These are the main activities we hold right now.

  • Japanese class

REN's story so far

From a poetry book to the work REN does today, each of these moments shaped the network it has become.

Support isn't only survival. It's room for children to simply play.

Our approach

Carrying tremendous experiences, they have the strength to look ahead and keep living.

Even so, because of various barriers, there are people who are placed in situations where it is hard to live as themselves.

REN respects each person's voice, feelings, and thoughts, and has kept creating places to learn and to play an active role together with them, thinking together, worrying together, laughing together, and supporting one another.

The refugee journey

One story, told simply

Every refugee's story is different. This is one way it can look: leaving home, a long crossing, and somewhere new to start again.

Leaving home

A bundle over one shoulder, and a last look back at everything familiar.

This story mentions danger and the loss of loved ones.

The first reason this person gives for leaving is simple: it was not safe. They had been looking for a way out since childhood. They entered Japan with a visa, and say it took about ten years, from the time they began looking for somewhere to flee to until now.

Japan Association for Refugees (JAR), “Not to survive, but to live my own life: an interview with a person recognized as a refugee” (in Japanese) · September 29, 2022

The crossing

Days or weeks at sea, or on foot. The journey itself is rarely simple.

This story mentions danger and the loss of loved ones.

Interviewed by UNHCR in 2010, Kate had fled Uganda for Kenya after her father was killed and her mother was taken, and had reached Kakuma refugee camp four years earlier. When she arrived, she thought, “This is going to be the end of me.”

UNHCR, “Q&A: Film helps empower young Ugandan refugee in Kenyan camp” (by Helene Caux) · March 18, 2010

Somewhere new

A door opens, and eventually there's room to just be a kid again.

Abebe waited five years from submitting the application to the first interview at the immigration office, and was recognized a year later: six years in all. During the application, Abebe could not find work that used their experience and washed dishes at an Italian restaurant. Today Abebe works at a Japanese company, using that experience.

Japan Association for Refugees (JAR), “Recognized after six years: giving up on returning to Ethiopia, this is now our home” (in Japanese) · November 13, 2025

A partnership with People Port Co., Ltd.'s "Omou Project." Conflict over the minerals used in computers has driven many people from their homes worldwide, including many of the refugees REN supports. Rather than let that hardware go to waste, we've partnered with a company that supports refugees through reuse and recycling: the buy-back value of collected devices is donated to REN.
