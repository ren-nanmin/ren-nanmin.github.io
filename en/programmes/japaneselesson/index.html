Japanese Lessons

Every Wednesday, 13:30–15:30.

What it is

For asylum seekers living in Japan, learning Japanese is an important opportunity that supports daily life and widens connections with society.

However, when the future is uncertain, even continuing to study can become difficult.

What is happening now? What will happen next? Who can I ask for advice when I am in trouble?

It is also important not to carry such worries alone, and to be able to talk with peace of mind and get the information one needs.

Centered on learning Japanese, REN continues efforts to support each person's daily life through various activities and partnerships.

REN holds exchange events and community activities so that Japanese people and refugees can share their cultures, accept their differences, and respect diversity. Learning the language is the first step toward living together, so REN has offered a Japanese class since 2013. After a pause during the pandemic, it fully reopened in May 2022 and welcomes everyone who comes, from infants to adults. It is more than a language class. It keeps building a place where refugees connect with one another and help one another grow. Today the weekly class has grown into a multinational community, mostly from Africa and the Middle East, with participants from more than 19 countries and an average of 35 people at each session.

Language is the single biggest barrier refugees face in settling into Japanese society. Beginners work with veteran teachers using textbooks and printed materials, building toward simple conversation. Intermediate and advanced students get free-ranging conversation practice with staff, aimed at understanding Japan more deeply, being able to talk about their home country and themselves, and picking up the Japanese they need for work. It's not only conversation: reading comprehension and writing are also offered on request. Students bring real intensity to it ("I want to study every day," "it's hard, but I'll keep at it"), and there's a genuine mutual-help culture where more experienced students coach newcomers.

Who it serves

Refugees and asylum seekers who want to learn Japanese, from complete beginners to advanced speakers.
